#+TITLE: Linear Algebra

Games create the illusion of continuous motion by displaying a sequence of snapshots of a virtual world over time. These snapshots are known as frames.

For each frame, the game constructs the scene using three fundamental elements: objects, lights, and a camera. These elements must be defined mathematically and placed within the scene, with each having properties that may change over time from one frame to the next. The value of each individual property depends not only on its initial value but also on values from previous frames.

Before a frame can be produced, the exact state of the entire world at that specific moment must be computed. The overall state of the scene is simply the combination of the states of all its elements. These states include properties like where each element is located, which way it's facing, how fast it's moving and in what direction, and other traits that determine how it behaves in the game.

** Algebraic Aspect
The state of the virtual world in each frame comes from simulating how objects interact with one another—for example, figuring out when and how they collide, how forces are transferred during a collision, and how the objects react afterward.

Every individual element's state is influenced by what happened in previous frames. These ongoing changes and interactions create complex relationships between all the elements—many things affecting each other at once, in ways that aren't simple to calculate one by one.

As a result, to keep the world consistent and realistic over time, large systems of algebraic equations must be solved for every single frame.

** Geometric Aspect
Objects in the virtual world are described mathematically by properties such as shape, size, position, and orientation. Together, these properties define the object’s geometry.

+ Geometry :: Geometry is a mathematical system based on axioms describing the relations between objects such as points, lines, and planes, independent of their physical interpretation.
  - David Hilbert – Foundations of Geometry (1899)

Light sources are defined in a similar geometric way. Their placement, direction, color, and strength determine how objects are lit up, creating shadows, highlights, and realistic appearances. Lighting is essential for making the scene look convincing, and there are many different techniques to model it (from simple to highly realistic). Lights can even have their own shapes, like a glowing bulb or a broad sunlight area.

Finally, the fully lit geometric scene is viewed through a camera—or, more precisely, projected onto the camera—which represents the player’s viewpoint. This projection transforms the scene into a two-dimensional image that is displayed on the screen.

** Combining Both
The mathematical tool set used to describe elements in scene and solve all of their interaction and relationships are is collectively known as linear algebra.

Geometry forms the foundation for how scenes are modeled, lit, and projected in games, while the algebra focuses on solving equations. Although geometry and algebra may appear to be separate and unrelated disciplines, they are deeply interconnected—truly two sides of the same coin, brought together and studied in linear algebra.

The word geometry literally means “earth measurement”. It comes from Ancient Greek: geo- (γῆ) meaning “earth” or “land,” combined with -metry (μετρία) from metron, meaning “measurement.” Thousands of years ago in Egypt and Mesopotamia, people developed the first geometric techniques to survey fields, redraw boundaries after floods, divide land fairly, and build monumental structures—essentially measuring the earth itself. The Greeks later took these practical methods and transformed them into a precise mathematical science, but they kept the original name γεωμετρία (geōmetría). That is why, even today, when we study points, lines, angles, shapes, and space in the abstract, we still call it geometry: a living reminder that one of the most theoretical branches of mathematics began with farmers and surveyors figuring out how to measure the land beneath their feet.
