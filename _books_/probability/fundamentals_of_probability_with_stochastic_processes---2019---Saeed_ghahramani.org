** 1.1 Introduction

*** Random, Certain, and Impossible Events

- *Random Event*: May or may not occur.
  - Example: A radioactive atom may or may not decay during a given time interval.
  - Example: An inspector may or may not find a defect in a product.

- *Certain Event*: Guaranteed to occur.
  - Example: Lightning is observed before thunder is heard.

- *Impossible Event*: Can never occur.
  - Example: An object traveling faster than the speed of light.

*** Why Probability Is Needed

Knowing that an event is random is not enough. We want to measure *how likely* it is to occur.

Observation:
- When an experiment is repeated many times, the proportion of occurrences of an event often approaches a constant value.
- Probability is used to describe this long-run behavior.

Example:
- Repeatedly tossing a fair coin results in the proportion of heads approaching 1/2.
- Therefore, P(Heads) = 1/2.

** 1.2 Sample Space and Events

*** Sample Space
- The *sample space* (S) is the set of all possible outcomes of an experiment.
- Each outcome is called a *sample point*.

Examples:
- Coin toss: S = {H, T}
- Lifetime of a light bulb: S = {x : x ≥ 0}
- Family genders, bus arrival times, round-off errors, etc.

*** Events
- An *event* is any subset of the sample space.
- An event occurs when the observed outcome belongs to that subset.

Examples:
- Coin toss: {H} = getting heads.
- Light bulb lifetime ≥ 100 hours.
- Bus arrives with 27 passengers between specific times.

*** Different Representations
- The same experiment can have different sample-space representations.
- Example:
  - Bus arrival time measured in hours.
  - Bus arrival time measured in minutes after 11:00.
- Different representations describe the same outcomes.

*** Basic Event Operations

**** Subset
- E ⊆ F means:
  - Whenever E occurs, F also occurs.

**** Equality
- E = F if:
  - E ⊆ F and F ⊆ E.

**** Intersection
- E ∩ F (or EF)
- Occurs when both E and F occur.

**** Union
- E ∪ F
- Occurs when at least one of E or F occurs.

**** Complement
- Eᶜ
- Occurs when E does not occur.

**** Difference
- E − F
- Occurs when E occurs but F does not.
- E − F = EFᶜ

**** Certain Event
- Event that always occurs.
- The sample space S itself.

**** Impossible Event
- Event that can never occur.
- The empty set ∅.

**** Mutually Exclusive Events
- Events that cannot occur together.
- E and F are mutually exclusive if:
  - E ∩ F = ∅

*** Operations on Collections of Events

**** Union of Many Events
- ⋃ Ei
- At least one Ei occurs.

**** Intersection of Many Events
- ⋂ Ei
- All Ei occur simultaneously.

*** Example: Airport Queue
Let:
- E = at least 5 planes waiting.
- F = at most 3 planes waiting.
- H = exactly 2 planes waiting.

Results:
- Eᶜ = at most 4 planes waiting.
- Fᶜ = at least 4 planes waiting.
- E ⊆ Fᶜ
- H ⊆ F
- E and F are mutually exclusive.
- E and H are mutually exclusive.

*** Venn Diagrams
- Useful for visualizing relationships among events.
- Helpful for intuition and counterexamples.
- Not considered rigorous proofs.

*** Event Algebra Laws

**** Basic Identities
- (Eᶜ)ᶜ = E
- E ∪ Eᶜ = S
- ES = E
- EEᶜ = ∅

**** Commutative Laws
- E ∪ F = F ∪ E
- EF = FE

**** Associative Laws
- E ∪ (F ∪ G) = (E ∪ F) ∪ G
- E(FG) = (EF)G

**** Distributive Laws
- (EF) ∪ H = (E ∪ H)(F ∪ H)
- (E ∪ F)H = EH ∪ FH

**** De Morgan's First Law
- (E ∪ F)ᶜ = EᶜFᶜ

**** De Morgan's Second Law
- (EF)ᶜ = Eᶜ ∪ Fᶜ

**** Useful Identity
- E = EF ∪ EFᶜ

*** Proof Technique: Elementwise Method
To prove two events are equal:

1. Show every point of the left side belongs to the right side.
2. Show every point of the right side belongs to the left side.

Then the two sets are equal.

Example:
- Proof of De Morgan's law:
  - (E ∪ F)ᶜ = EᶜFᶜ

*** Main Takeaways
- Probability theory is built on sample spaces and events.
- Events are simply sets of outcomes.
- Set operations (union, intersection, complement, difference) describe relationships between events.
- Venn diagrams aid intuition.
- Event algebra identities and De Morgan's laws are fundamental tools used throughout probability.

** Additional Concepts Introduced in Exercises of section 1.2

*** Finite vs Infinite Sample Spaces
- A sample space may contain:
  - Finite outcomes (coin tosses, dice rolls).
  - Countably infinite outcomes (flip until first heads).
  - Uncountably infinite outcomes (choose a real number from an interval).

Examples:
- Flip until first heads:
  S = {H, TH, TTH, TTTH, ...}
- Choose a point from (-1,1):
  S = {x : -1 < x < 1}

*** Ordered Outcomes
- Sometimes order matters.

Examples:
- Three coin tosses:
  HHT ≠ HTH ≠ THH

- Arranging books on a shelf:
  Different orders correspond to different outcomes.

*** Cartesian Product Sample Spaces
- When an experiment consists of several measurements, outcomes are often represented as tuples.

Examples:
- Two dice:
  S = {(i,j): 1 ≤ i,j ≤ 6}

- Bus example:
  S = {(passengers,time)}

*** System Reliability Representation
- Components of a system can be represented by events.

Let:
- Ai = component i works.

Then:
- System works = logical combination of Ai's.
- System failure = complement of the working event.

This idea appears repeatedly later in probability and engineering.

*** Exactly One / Exactly Two / At Least One
Common event constructions:

- At least one:
  A ∪ B ∪ C

- None:
  AᶜBᶜCᶜ

- Exactly one:
  ABᶜCᶜ ∪ AᶜBCᶜ ∪ AᶜBᶜC

- Exactly two:
  ABCᶜ ∪ ABᶜC ∪ AᶜBC

These appear frequently in exercises.

*** Partition of an Event
Events can be decomposed into disjoint pieces.

Example:
- E = EF ∪ EFᶜ

The two pieces:
- EF
- EFᶜ

are mutually exclusive.

This idea later leads to the Law of Total Probability.

*** Infinite Unions and Intersections
For a sequence of events E₁,E₂,...

- ⋃∞ᵢ₌₁ Ei
  means at least one Ei occurs.

- ⋂∞ᵢ₌₁ Ei
  means all Ei occur.

Example:
Ei = (-1/2ⁱ, 1/2ⁱ)

Then:
- ⋃∞ᵢ₌₁ Ei = (-1/2, 1/2)
- ⋂∞ᵢ₌₁ Ei = {0}

*** Event Sequences
Many exercises define:

A₁, A₂, A₃, ...

and ask about:

- infinitely many Ai occur
- eventually an Ai occurs
- all Ai occur

This prepares for limit concepts in later chapters.

*** Mutually Exclusive vs Complementary
Mutually Exclusive:
- A ∩ B = ∅

Complementary:
- A ∩ B = ∅
- A ∪ B = S

Every pair of complementary events is mutually exclusive,
but not every mutually exclusive pair is complementary.

*** Sample Space Design
A major skill in probability is choosing a useful sample space.

The same experiment may have different valid sample spaces.

Example:
Families with three children

Detailed:
- {BBB, BBG, BGB, GBB, BGG, GBG, GGB, GGG}

Compressed:
- {0 girls, 1 girl, 2 girls, 3 girls}

Different representations may lead to different probabilities for sample points.

*** Event Translation Skill
One of the most important abilities in probability:

English statement → Set expression

Examples:

- "At least one occurs"
  → A ∪ B ∪ C

- "None occurs"
  → AᶜBᶜCᶜ

- "A occurs but B does not"
  → ABᶜ

- "A or B but not both"
  → ABᶜ ∪ AᶜB

- "Exactly one occurs"
  → ABᶜ ∪ AᶜB

- "Both occur"
  → AB
